Does a declarative rewrite remove the failure or only reduce variance?

Does a declarative rewrite remove the failure or only reduce variance?

Concern: the audit and both sibling files recommend rewriting prohibitions into declarative form (X: disabled). Does a verified source show that this removes the failure, or only reduces cross-linguistic variance?

Status: only the weaker claim is supported. The mitigation may be doing nothing about the mechanism the tier table is built on.

the source

arXiv:2603.25015 — Imperative Interference: Social Register Shapes Instruction Topology in Large Language Models, Mason, Tony (single author), 2026-03-26, cs.CL / cs.AI / cs.SE. PDF in ../raw/.

What it measures: instruction-level ablation across four languages × four models.

  • English imperative instructions cooperate; Spanish imperative instructions compete — same semantic content, opposite interaction topology
  • the inversion is mediated by social register: imperative mood carries different obligatory force across speech communities
  • declarative rewriting of one instruction block reduces cross-linguistic variance by 81% (p = 0.029, permutation test)
  • rewriting three of eleven imperative blocks flips Spanish topology from competitive to cooperative, with spillover onto unrewritten blocks
  • corpus: 22 hand-authored probes against a production system prompt of 56 blocks
  • the paper's own framing: "NEVER do X" is an exercise of authority whose force is language-dependent; "X: disabled" is a factual description that transfers

what it does not measure

It does not measure violation rate. It measures variance across languages. Those are different quantities:

  • 81% variance reduction = the rewrite makes languages behave more like each other
  • failure removal = the rewritten instruction is complied with more often

A rewrite could cut cross-linguistic variance to near zero while compliance stayed exactly as low in every language, and the headline figure would be unchanged. Nothing in the abstract reports a compliance rate.

why this matters here

The gotcha tier's claim is that prohibitions fail — that failure is what the 87.5% priming figure is measuring, and what the (unbacked) 40% threshold was offered as independent support for. If the declarative mitigation only equalises behaviour across languages, it does not address a failure that is already uniform.

Mason's own framing is more modest than the audit's use of it: a register effect, language-dependent, with an inference the paper marks as a testable prediction rather than a result — "constitutional AI principles authored in imperative mood may create language-dependent alignment."

the compounding problem

arXiv:2601.07354 (MetaGlyph, van Gassen) measures declarative symbolic notation on the same axis and finds it is not free: ∈ operator fidelity spans 26%–91% across eight models, and ∩ composition fails in all but one. So the replacement form has a measured fidelity spread of its own.

That is not a refutation of the mitigation — the two papers measure different things. It does mean the mitigation is a trade, and the audit presents it as a free fix.

what would settle it

An arm with prohibitions and a matched arm with X: disabled substitutions, both scored for compliance rate, not variance. Per the rewriter's substitution set as a declared treatment.
